Quitting my job to be a student again

August 2026

I’m quitting my job this month. It pays well, the people are great, and I’m leaving anyway, to be a student again.

I studied maths and statistics, and I loved most of it. I thought about staying for a master’s and a PhD back home in Badajoz. But I had just graduated and what I really wanted was to make money. “Do what you love and you’ll never work a day in your life” is nice advice from people whose intellectual pursuits happen to satisfy its material needs. Not my case.

So when I got an offer from a big company in a country with proper salaries, I took it without thinking twice. The work was reasonably aligned with what I’d studied, it helped me do a decent piggy bank, which buys optionality, something pretty useful for a 22yo.

I don’t regret it. But it didn’t take long to see that this world wasn’t mine. The problems weren’t intellectually challenging nor engaging and the rest of my hours went to writing documents for non-technical audiences, managing stakeholders, sitting in meetings…

What actually settled it was looking at my managers. Good people, competent, well paid. And doing even less technical work than me. Every level up the ladder, the maths got further away. I was staring at my own future, and I couldn’t picture myself there in forty years. The jobs where you do get to think hard about hard problems all had the same sign on the door: PhD required.

Here’s the thing though. It would be easy to tell myself a story about passion and calling. The more honest evidence is what I was already doing without noticing: coming home from work and opening a different laptop to do research. I wrote a paper on exact methods for the Orienteering Problem, built the whole solver in C++, sent it to a journal (still under review, fingers crossed).

So: in September I start the MVA master’s at ENS Paris-Saclay. The plan is to keep pulling the optimal transport thread and find the right PhD. I know exactly how much money I’m leaving on the table. The savings from these years exist precisely for this. And the bet is asymmetric anyway: if research doesn’t work out, industry will still be there. If I’d stayed, the research door would have kept closing a little more every year, and I think at some point I would have stopped noticing.

To my colleagues: thank you, sincerely. You made this short chapter a good one!
